### What We're Investigating

A mathematical framework for LLM-based autograding that uses:

- **Quality Dimensions (QDs)**: Binary features extracted from student response corpus
- **Formal Concept Analysis (FCA)**: Creates equivalence classes and partial ordering
- **Concept-Fidelity**: Total ordering within equivalence classes
- **Betweenness**: Geometric operator to position new texts and induce rubrics



## Part 1: Framework Theory

### 1.1 The Fundamental Problem

**Core Philosophical Claim (Page 2 of paper):**

> "We need to accept that the classifier will necessarily be non-objective. Indeed, even the classes and criteria that define them are dependent on many non-objective and vaguely defined ideas such as 'instructor expectations'."
> 

Objective grading for free-form written answers is impossible. Different instructors with different pedagogical goals will grade the same answer differently, and all are "correct" for their context.

**The Latent Variable Problem (Page 2):**

> "We want to classify an answer based on actual understanding, which is a latent property that is inaccessible to us. We instead classify the answer based on an output, that may or may not accurately reflect this latent property."
> 

We can only observe what students write (output), not what they understand (latent mental state). 

Two sources of error:

1. **Encoding error**: Student understands but cannot express clearly
2. **Decoding error**: Grader misinterprets student's meaning

**Example:**

- **Question:** "What is voltage?"
- **Student's mental model (latent)**: Perfect understanding of voltage as electrical potential difference
- **Student's written output**: "Voltage is like the pressure of electricity"
- **Grader A interpretation**: "Wrong—voltage isn't pressure" → 40%
- **Grader B interpretation**: "Good analogy showing conceptual grasp" → 85%
- **Problem**: We cannot know which interpretation is correct from text alone

### 1.2 Quality Dimensions: The Core Construct

**Definition (Page 3):**

> "A set of natural language features that may be present or absent in a given exemplar. These quality dimensions are necessarily subjective, often defined by the cognitive scientist and based on their lived experience, cultural learning, and societal norms."
> 

**Key Properties:**

- **Binary**: Each QD is either present (1) or absent (0) in a text
- **Semantic**: Defined in natural language, not as vectors
- **LLM-extracted**: Generated by LLM from corpus, not instructor-specified
- **Subjective**: Reflect LLM's understanding of corpus structure

**Why reject embeddings? (Page 3):**

> "We want to avoid imposing numerical concepts such as 'distance' on a corpus, because doing so simply shifts the question of 'what does similarity mean' to 'how do you convert natural language into a numerical representation'."
> 

Embeddings are arbitrary mappings that lose semantic interpretability. We want similarity defined in natural language, not Euclidean distance.

**Example: Voltage Question**

- **QD_1**: "mentions energy"
- **QD_2**: "mentions charge"
- **QD_3**: "uses ratio/relationship structure"
- **QD_4**: "discusses units"
- **QD_5**: "provides examples"

**Text A:** "Voltage is energy per charge" → {1, 1, 1, 0, 0}
**Text B:** "Voltage is measured in volts" → {0, 0, 0, 1, 0}
**Text C:** "Voltage is like water pressure in pipes" → {0, 0, 1, 0, 1}

### 1.3 Extracting Quality Dimensions (The Algorithm)

**Iterative Refinement Process (Page 4):**

**Step 1:** Provide LLM with **seed set of QDs** and instruct it to analyze corpus exemplars according to these dimensions

- Seed set need not be complete or accurate
- Example seed: {mentions energy, mentions charge, discusses mechanism}

**Step 2:** Provide LLM with its own output from Step 1 and instruct it to **critique and suggest improvements**

- LLM might say: "energy and charge always appear together in correct answers—should be merged"
- Or: "missing dimension: temporal dynamics (how voltage changes over time)"

**Step 3:** Provide LLM with current QD list and feedback, instruct it to **revise the QD set**

- LLM produces new QD list
- Might merge: {energy, charge} → {energy-per-charge relationship}
- Might split: {mechanism} → {mechanical operation, electrical operation}

**Repeat Steps 1-3 until stopping criterion met**

**Empirical finding (Page 4):**

> "In our experiments we have found that 2-3 iterations produces an adequate and comprehensive set of quality dimensions that captures an acceptable amount of the variation in the corpus."
> 

**Operations during refinement:**

- **Add**: Discover new dimensions corpus reveals
- **Drop**: Remove dimensions that don't capture meaningful variation (present in all texts or no texts)
- **Split**: Decompose overly broad dimensions into finer-grained ones
- **Merge**: Combine coupled dimensions into compound QDs

### 1.4 Formal Concept Analysis and Equivalence Classes

**FCA Process (Page 4):**

> "Review texts in the corpus and determine, for each quality dimension, whether it is present or absent in a given text."
> 

**Equivalence Class Definition (Page 4):**

> "A text T is a member of an equivalence class E if the set of quality dimensions present in T is also present in all other texts in E."
> 

**Formal notation:**

- Let D = {d₁, d₂, ..., dₙ} be the set of quality dimensions
- Let T be a text
- Define QD_profile(T) = {dᵢ ∈ D : dᵢ is present in T}
- Equivalence class: E_D' = {T : QD_profile(T) = D'}

**Example:**

- **D** = {energy, charge, ratio, units}
- **Text 1**: "Energy per charge" → QD_profile = {energy, charge, ratio}
- **Text 2**: "Charge to energy ratio" → QD_profile = {energy, charge, ratio}
- **Text 3**: "Energy divided by charge, measured in volts" → QD_profile = {energy, charge, ratio, units}

**Equivalence classes:**

- E_{energy,charge,ratio} = {Text 1, Text 2}
- E_{energy,charge,ratio,units} = {Text 3}

**Partial Ordering (Page 4):**

> "Some equivalence classes have sets of quality dimensions that are proper subsets of others. It is under this proper subset operation (⊂) that we may partially order the equivalence classes."
> 

**Formal definition:**

- E_1 ⊂ E_2 if D_1 ⊂ D_2
- This creates a lattice structure

**Example lattice:**

```
E_{energy,charge,ratio,units}
         |
E_{energy,charge,ratio}    E_{charge,ratio,units}
         |                           |
    E_{energy,charge}           E_{ratio,units}
         |                           |
    E_{energy}                  E_{ratio}
         |                           |
           E_∅ (null—no QDs)

```

**Critical Note (Page 5):**

> "It is important to note that quality dimensions are chosen to capture the variation in the corpus texts. Crucially, they are not chosen with any regard to whether the texts correctly, or even adequately, answer the question for which they are responses. Therefore, the partial ordering does not on its own imply anything about correctness."
> 

**QD structure is about variation, not quality. The partial ordering tells us which texts have more/fewer dimensions, not which are better.**

### 1.5 The Voltage Example: Structure Without Correctness

**The Problem Case (Page 5):**

**Question:** "What is voltage?"

**Corpus texts:**

1. "Energy per unit charge" → D₁ = {energy, charge, ratio}
2. "Power per amp" → D₂ = {energy, charge, ratio, current}
3. "Coulombs per second" → D₃ = {charge, ratio, current}

**Partial ordering:**

- D₁ ⊂ D₂ (Text 2 has more dimensions)
- D₃ ⊂ D₂ (Text 2 has most dimensions)

**But pedagogically:**

- Text 1: **CORRECT** (standard definition)
- Text 2: **PARTIALLY CORRECT** (valid via V=P/I, but unconventional)
- Text 3: **INCORRECT** (confusing charge flow rate with voltage)

**The problem:** Text 2 has the broadest QD set (D₂) but is the least conventional answer. The partial ordering doesn't capture correctness.

**This is Board 1's diagram:** Structure can be anywhere between min acceptable and max possible, but correctness is separate.

### 1.6 Necessity, Admissibility, and Sufficiency

**Introduced to bridge structure → correctness gap (Pages 5-7):**

**Necessity (Page 5):**

> "An equivalence class has the property of necessity if a correct answer to the assessment must have structure in all of that equivalence class's quality dimensions."
> 

**If a QD is necessary, correct answers MUST have it.**

**Example:** For voltage, {energy, charge, ratio} might be necessary—correct answers must discuss energy-per-charge relationship.

**Three Problems with Necessity:**

**Problem 1: Structure ≠ Correctness (Page 6)**

> "A text may have structure along all necessary quality dimensions but still be wrong. For example, 'Voltage is charge per unit energy' is incorrect, but still has structure in all necessary quality dimensions."
> 

**Example:**

- Necessary: {energy, charge, ratio}
- Text: "Voltage is charge per unit energy"
- Has structure in: {energy ✓, charge ✓, ratio ✓}
- But: **WRONG** (backwards—should be energy per charge)

**Problem 2: Multiple Valid Equivalence Classes (Page 6)**

> "Many assessments admit correct answers that are not in the same equivalence class."
> 

**Example:**

- Text A: "Voltage is energy per unit charge" → {energy, charge, ratio}
- Text B: "Voltage is a potential difference" → {potential, difference, electric_field}
- Both are **CORRECT** but have different QD sets

**Problem 3: Irrelevant Dimensions with Errors (Pages 6-7)**

> "Some equivalence classes may contain the entirety of the necessary subset of quality dimensions, along with additional quality dimensions... It is possible to give an incorrect statement along an irrelevant quality dimension. Does that make the whole text incorrect?"
> 

**Example:**

- Necessary: {energy, charge, ratio}
- Text 1: "Energy per unit charge, measured in Amps" → {energy, charge, ratio, units}
    - Units dimension: **INCORRECT** (Amps is current, not voltage)
    - But energy-per-charge part: **CORRECT**
    - Is the whole answer wrong?

**New Concepts to Address Problems:**

**Admissibility (Page 6):**

> "A quality dimension is admissible if its presence may contribute to a correct answer."
> 

An admissible QD can appear in correct answers but isn't required.

**Projection (Page 6):**

> "A text in equivalence class E₁ is projected to a class E₂ where D₁ ⊂ D₂, by removing its structure along all dimensions d not in D₁ ∩ D₂."
> 

Remove extra dimensions to see if core answer is still correct.

**Example:**

- Text: "Energy per charge, measured in Amps"
- QD set: {energy, charge, ratio, units}
- Project onto {energy, charge, ratio} (remove units)
- Result: "Energy per charge"
- Correctness after projection: **CORRECT**
- Therefore: units dimension was **irrelevant** to core correctness

**Admissible Projection (Page 6):**

> **"A projection is admissible if, after projection, the correctness of the projected text is not changed."**
> 

**Irrelevant Dimensions (Page 6):**

> **"For a given text, the irrelevant dimensions are all residual dimensions corresponding to admissible projections."**
> 

**Sufficient Equivalence Class (Page 6):**

> **"An equivalence class is sufficient if it is admissible and it is possible to create correct answers using only its quality dimensions."**
> 

A sufficient class has enough dimensions to enable complete correct answers (but texts in that class may still be wrong).

### 1.7 Defining Correctness

**Correctness Criteria (Page 7):**

A text is **correct** when:

1. It is a member of a **sufficient equivalence class**
2. Contains **only correct statements** for every quality dimension in that class

**Partial Correctness:**

> "A text belonging to an admissible, but not sufficient, equivalence class is therefore partially correct with missing details."
> 

**Incorrect Texts:**

> "A text is incorrect whenever it contains an incorrect statement along any of its quality dimension."
> 

**Nuanced Handling (Page 7):**

> "Texts may be partially correct with errors if they contain a mixture of correct and incorrect statements. The exact distinction is left to the instructor giving the assessment."
> 

Framework acknowledges mixtures but provides no formal handling—defers to instructor judgment.

**This is Board 2's problem:** How to handle mixtures of correct and incorrect statements?

### 1.8 LLM as Judge of Correctness

**The Consistency vs. Correctness Problem (Pages 7-8):**

**Acknowledgment of limitations:**

> "Judging correctness cannot be objective, and nuance and unexpected answers may still cause errors."
> 

**Shift in focus:**

> "Perhaps, then, we should focus on whether a grader, whether human or AI, is at least consistent in their judgments."
> 

**But consistency has problems too:**

> "Humans may tire or forget choices they made, so the same correct answer may be marked as incorrect simply by mistake. LLMs are certainly not immune to this."
> 

**The provisional nature (Page 8):**

> "We state that judgments of correctness are, at best, provisional, and in a robust grading system the process of giving and receiving feedback should be a two-way conversation between the teaching team and the students."
> 

**Proceeding assumption:**

> "We continue under the assumption that the grader in use is at least very likely to produce proper judgment."
> 

**Critical insight from literature review:** Consistency and correctness are **orthogonal properties**:

- A grader can be consistently wrong (high reproducibility, low accuracy)
- A grader can be correct on average but inconsistent (low reproducibility, varies across trials)
- Need to validate BOTH independently

### 1.9 Concept-Fidelity

**Definition (Page 9):**

A text has high **concept-fidelity** in an equivalence class when it is:

1. **Correct**
2. **Reflects the full intended meaning of the assessment**

**The vagueness acknowledged:**

> "Once again we encounter a term that cannot be rigorously defined in general. The 'intended meaning' is an emergent property of a text that depends on the intersection of the expectations placed on the students, the personal beliefs of the instructor, and the judgment capabilities of the grader."
> 

**Grader-specific nature (Page 9):**

> "Concept fidelity is necessarily understood to imply that the rating of concept fidelity is according to a specific grader, rather than some objective property."
> 

**The consistency claim:**

> "We argue here that, as long as the same grader is used for all texts, we may rank those texts on concept fidelity in a consistent manner."
> 

Concept-fidelity is the LLM's subjective assessment of "how good" an answer is within its equivalence class. Can't be objective, but can be consistent if same LLM used.

**Example:**
Within E_{energy,charge,ratio}:

- Text A: "Energy per charge"
- Text B: "Energy divided by charge"
- Text C: "The ratio of energy to charge"
- Text D: "Energy per unit charge, representing electrical potential"

**Concept-fidelity ranking (hypothetical LLM judgment):**

1. Text D (most complete and precise)
2. Text A (concise and standard)
3. Text C (correct but wordy)
4. Text B (correct but less clear)

### 1.10 Total Ordering via Concept-Fidelity

**Algorithm for Total Ordering (Pages 9-10):**

**Given:** Set of texts in same equivalence class

**Procedure:**

1. Choose the **one text with highest concept-fidelity** from the set
2. Label that text and **remove it** from the set
3. **Repeat** steps 1-2 until no texts remain

**The order of extraction is the total ordering.**

**Example:**

- Start: {Text A, Text B, Text C, Text D}
- Round 1: LLM picks Text D (highest) → remove → Ordering: [D]
- Round 2: LLM picks Text A → remove → Ordering: [D, A]
- Round 3: LLM picks Text C → remove → Ordering: [D, A, C]
- Round 4: Only B remains → Ordering: [D, A, C, B]

**Result:** D > A > C > B (in concept-fidelity)

**Scope Limitation (Page 10):**

> "We can impose this total ordering on the set of texts that were submitted. We do not require that this total ordering also be applicable to new submissions as yet."
> 

 This ordering is for the current corpus only. New texts need to be positioned within this ordering (that's where betweenness comes in).

### 1.11 Betweenness

**Definition (Page 10):**

**Betweenness operator:**

```
B(a, b, c) → {true, false}

```

When B(a,b,c) is true, we say **b is between a and c**.

**Application via concept-fidelity:**

> "Let a, b, c be three texts from the same equivalence class. Then B(a, b, c) is true whenever b's concept fidelity is no lower than c's and no higher than a's, presuming the concept fidelity of a is higher than that of c."
> 

**Formal definition:**

```
B(a, b, c) = true  ⟺  concept_fidelity(c) ≤ concept_fidelity(b) ≤ concept_fidelity(a)

```

**Purpose (Page 11):**

> "Where this operator is useful is when new texts are introduced. When we introduce a new text, we may not want to disrupt the original total ordering by recomputing it. Instead, we may simply want to locate this new text within the total order."
> 

**How it works:**

- Given: Original total ordering [a₁, a₂, a₃, ..., aₙ] (high to low concept-fidelity)
- Given: New text b
- Find: Position in ordering where b belongs

**Algorithm:**

1. Find largest i where concept_fidelity(aᵢ) ≥ concept_fidelity(b)
2. Find smallest j where concept_fidelity(b) ≥ concept_fidelity(aⱼ)
3. Position: aᵢ ≥ b ≥ aⱼ (b is between aᵢ and aⱼ)

**Special Cases (Page 11):**

**Case 1: b lower than all original texts**

- B(a,b,c) = false for all a,c in original set
- Introduce **NULL** (null text with no concept-fidelity)
- Always: B(a, b, NULL) = true for any a

**Case 2: b higher than all original texts**

- B(a,b,c) = false for all a,c in original set
- b exceeds all references

**Important note (Page 11):**

> "Because our definition of B(a,b,c) allows for b's concept fidelity to be equal to that of some text in the original set, the Betweenness operation induces a pre-order on the set of texts."
> 

Ties are allowed—multiple texts can have same concept-fidelity.

### 1.12 Explainable Grading via Betweenness

**The Rubric Problem (Page 11):**

Traditional rubrics suffer from:

1. **Borderline cases** may be classified differently depending on grader and wording
2. **Rubrics may contain conflicting criteria** or may be missing critical dimensions

**The Alternative Approach (Pages 11-12):**

Instead of explicit rubric, use **exemplar-based rubric induction**:

**Simple Example:**

- **Text a**: High-quality solution (meets/exceeds expectations)
- **Text c**: Partial solution (below expectations but non-null)
- **NULL**: No understanding

**Total ordering:** [a, c, NULL]

**Grading new text b via betweenness:**

- **If B(a,b,c) = true**: b is between a and c → **approaches expectations**
- **If B(a,b,*) = false for all texts AND b > a**: b exceeds a → **exceeds expectations**
- **If B(c,b,NULL) = true**: b is between c and null → **does not meet expectations**

**Key Insight (Page 12):**

> "By creating such a set of texts and using betweenness, we may induce a rubric without explicitly listing all criteria and levels against which we are evaluating texts."
> 

**Flexibility:**

- Can use 2 reference texts (meets/doesn't meet)
- Can use 3+ reference texts (more granular: excellent/good/acceptable/poor)
- Can use instructor-generated texts OR high-quality student work OR hybrid

**The Method's Essence (Page 12):**

> "This method of rubric induction can be performed based on potential solutions only. We do not need to define and explain criteria, we only need to identify sample texts that have varying degrees of concept fidelity."
> 

**Feedback to students:**
"Your answer is between our 'acceptable' and 'excellent' examples. Compared to the excellent example, your answer lacks [specific detail]. See the excellent example for how to improve."
